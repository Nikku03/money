# A primer on how markets and people work

General knowledge for one question: will a stock beat SPY (the S&P 500) over the next few weeks? It holds no facts about any company. Facts about the stock come only from the snapshot or the tools.

## How prices form

- A price is the market's estimate of a company's future cash flows, discounted at the return investors require, and moves when either changes. Expectations move prices, not results: a record quarter can send a stock down when more was expected, and a loss can send it up when less was feared.
- Public news is priced quickly. Clear news (earnings, guidance, a merger, a regulatory decision) is mostly in the price within minutes to a day (Fama, Fisher, Jensen and Roll 1969; Fama 1970). Anything in a snapshot was public by that day's close, so the price already reflects it.
- A stock's move is the market's move plus its own. Its return is roughly beta times the market's return plus a part specific to the company. Whether it beats SPY depends on that specific part, plus beta minus one times the market's move: in a rising market a high-beta stock tends to beat SPY, in a falling one to trail it.
- Over a week, a stock's expected return relative to SPY is close to zero and small next to how much it varies.

## What moves a stock

- Earnings against expectations: the surprise, not the level. Guidance about coming quarters often matters more than the quarter just reported.
- Interest rates and risk appetite. Rising rates and falling appetite for risk hurt stocks whose value lies in distant cash flows (fast growers, companies not yet profitable) more than others.
- Liquidity and flows: index additions, fund inflows and outflows, forced selling, buybacks and share issues. Price moves from flows alone often fade.
- The industry and the factors it belongs to. Much of what looks like a stock's own move is its sector's.
- Uncertainty. Around earnings, the size of a move is more predictable than its direction.

## Base rates

- In any week or month, about half of stocks beat SPY, on average a little under half, because returns are skewed: a few large winners pull the index average above the typical stock. The share varies a lot from week to week. When a few large stocks lead the index most stocks trail it; in a broad rally most beat it.
- Over long horizons most single stocks do worse than the market: about 4% of US listed companies accounted for all the market's wealth creation above Treasury bills from 1926 to 2016, and most stocks returned less than one-month bills over their lives (Bessembinder 2018).
- Typical moves follow from volatility. A stock whose daily returns vary by s moves about s times the square root of h over h sessions. About two times in three the move lies within one such width, and about nine times in ten within 1.65 widths, with fatter tails than a normal curve. A stock with 30% annual volatility (about 1.9% a day) moves more than 4% in a week about one week in three.
- Known patterns, where they survive, are worth a few tenths of a percent a month on average across many stocks. Against a weekly spread of 2% to 4% for one stock against the index, even a real edge moves the chance of beating SPY by a point or two.
- Confident calls are usually wrong. Most professional fund managers do not beat their benchmarks after costs, and the few who do rarely keep doing so (Carhart 1997; Fama and French 2010). Expert forecasters in many fields are poorly calibrated: they say 80% when they are right far less often (Tetlock 2005).

## Known patterns and how they faded

Each comes from published research, and most weakened after publication as investors traded on them.

- **Momentum.** Stocks that rose most over the past year, skipping the latest month, kept beating the losers for 3 to 12 months (Jegadeesh and Titman 1993; Carhart 1997). It fails abruptly in sharp market rebounds, when past losers jump (Daniel and Moskowitz 2016).
- **Short-term reversal.** Last week's and last month's losers tend to beat last month's winners the next month (Jegadeesh 1990; Lehmann 1990). Much of this is a reward for supplying liquidity, and little survives trading costs in large stocks.
- **Long-term reversal.** Losers of the past three to five years beat the winners of those years (De Bondt and Thaler 1985).
- **Value.** Stocks cheap on earnings, book value or cash flow earned more on average (Basu 1977; Fama and French 1992; Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny 1994). The premium has been weak for long stretches in large US stocks.
- **Profitability and investment.** Firms with high gross profit over assets (Novy-Marx 2013), low accruals (Sloan 1996) and slow asset growth (Cooper, Gulen and Schill 2008) earned more.
- **Low volatility.** Low-risk and low-beta stocks earned as much as risky ones or more (Ang, Hodrick, Xing and Zhang 2006; Baker, Bradley and Wurgler 2011; Frazzini and Pedersen 2014).
- **Post-earnings drift.** After a large earnings surprise, prices kept drifting the same way for weeks (Ball and Brown 1968; Bernard and Thomas 1989). The drift is much smaller in large, liquid stocks (Chordia, Goyal, Sadka, Sadka and Shivakumar 2009).
- **52-week high.** Stocks near their 52-week high did better than those far below it: investors anchor on the high and react too little to good news near it (George and Hwang 2004).
- **Lottery stocks.** Stocks with extreme one-day gains in the past month tended to do worse afterwards, because some investors overpay for a small chance of a big win (Barberis and Huang 2008; Bali, Cakici and Whitelaw 2011).
- **Insider buying.** Open-market purchases by officers and directors predicted somewhat higher returns, mostly in small stocks and when the purchase breaks the insider's usual routine. Insider sales say little: insiders sell for many reasons (Seyhun 1986; Lakonishok and Lee 2001; Cohen, Malloy and Pomorski 2012).
- **Attention.** Stocks in the news or trading on unusual volume draw buying by individuals (Barber and Odean 2008); prices may rise for a short while and then give the gain back (Da, Engelberg and Gao 2011). Other samples found a lasting premium after high-volume weeks (Gervais, Kaniel and Mingelgrin 2001). The evidence is mixed.

**After publication.** McLean and Pontiff (2016) studied 97 published patterns: their returns were about 26% lower outside the original samples and about 58% lower after publication. Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue that so many patterns have been tried that a new one needs a t-statistic above 3, not 2. Many published patterns do not replicate once very small stocks are treated properly (Hou, Xue and Zhang, 2017 working paper). In recent decades only a few company traits add independent information about monthly returns (Green, Hand and Zhang 2017), and patterns weakened as trading became cheaper and faster (Chordia, Subrahmanyam and Tong 2014).

## Human nature

- **Overconfidence.** People overrate the precision of what they know, trade too much and earn less for it (Odean 1999; Barber and Odean 2000; Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam 1998).
- **Herding and extrapolation.** Investors chase recent winners, money flows follow past returns, and feedback between prices and enthusiasm builds bubbles and crashes (De Long, Shleifer, Summers and Waldmann 1990; Shiller 2000).
- **Loss aversion and the disposition effect.** Losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains please (Kahneman and Tversky 1979; Tversky and Kahneman 1992). Investors sell winners too soon and hold losers too long (Shefrin and Statman 1985; Odean 1998), which can slow the price's response to news (Grinblatt and Han 2005; Frazzini 2006).
- **Anchoring.** People lean on a reference number, such as a past price, the 52-week high, a round number or an analyst's target, and adjust too little from it (Tversky and Kahneman 1974).
- **Recency and availability.** Recent and vivid events weigh more than they should.
- **Narrative.** A good story makes a forecast feel surer without making it more accurate (Shiller 2017).
- **Overreaction and underreaction.** Investors react too little to ordinary news that spreads slowly, such as an earnings report (Hong and Stein 1999), and too much to a long run of good or bad news (Barberis, Shleifer and Vishny 1998). Which one is at work in a given case is hard to tell in advance.
- A model reasoning in words shares these habits: a fluent explanation is not evidence.

## How to reason

1. **Start from the base rate.** The chance a stock beats SPY is about 0.50, a little under on average.
2. **Ask what is already priced in.** Every fact in the snapshot was public by its date's close and is already in the price. A strong quarter, a big rally or bad news is not by itself a reason to expect more of the same.
3. **Move only for evidence with a tested record,** and only as far as that record supports. A pattern that failed this bank's tests (below) deserves almost no weight, and a story deserves none.
4. **Keep most probabilities between 0.45 and 0.55; 0.60 is a strong view.** If a stock's return against SPY over a week varies by 3%, a chance of 0.60 means expecting it to beat SPY by about 0.75% that week, around 40% a year: more than any known pattern has delivered. Beyond 0.35 or 0.65 is almost never justified for one stock over a week or a month.
5. **A model's forecast, when one is given, is one input.** The learner (F1) weighs the patterns above from history. It carries weight only as far as its own record shows skill, and it is usually close to 0.50 too.
6. **When nothing stands out, say so** and answer close to 0.50. That is the usual answer, and an honest one.

## This bank's own tests

Each idea was tested as a monthly portfolio against an equal-weight universe of US stocks, first on TRAIN months (2011 to 2018), then on later unseen months.

- On TRAIN months two ideas looked promising: 12-1 momentum among 60 large stocks, +0.22% a month (p = 0.0148), and insider buying among 741 stocks, +0.11% a month (p = 0.0080). Short-term reversal (+0.05% a month, p = 0.0722) and a ridge model combining several signals (+0.14% a month, p = 0.0546) were weaker. Earnings yield, low asset growth, the earnings reaction and SUE earned nothing or less on TRAIN months.
- On the later unseen months every hypothesis failed its TEST gate: none beat a shuffled signal after costs and a correction for testing many ideas. Working in the sample it was found in and failing afterwards is the common case.
- The universe is chosen by today's market value, so it holds only survivors. That flatters stocks that were small or fast-growing in the past and hurts value.
- Treat every pattern above as unproven here.

## What not to do

- Never use knowledge of events after the snapshot date: later prices, earnings, deals, scandals, index changes, splits, or anything you remember about how the company fared. If you recognise the company and remember what happened next, set it aside. The forecast must be one that could have been made on that date.
- Use only numbers from the snapshot or the tools. The numbers in this primer are general background, not facts about the stock.
- Never infer anything about private individuals. Executives appear only in their public roles (filings, Form 4s, earnings calls); do not guess who someone is, where they go or why.
- Do not move far from 0.50 on one fact, and do not mistake a reason that sounds good for evidence.
